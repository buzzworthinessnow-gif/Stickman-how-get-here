# Pilot Episode — How Did We Get Here? The Car

**Status:** illustrative research and production outline; **not rendered**. Verify specific claims and dates with reliable sources before using them in narration. This is a sample of the workflow, not a claim that all “firsts” are settled.

**Working title:** *How Did We Get Here? From Steam Wagons to Electric Cars*

**Hook idea:** A modern car can practically drive itself through traffic. But the journey began when inventors were trying to make a carriage move **without a horse**. How did an awkward experiment become the machine that reshaped cities?

**Causal spine:** We went from animal-powered travel to modern automobiles because inventors developed self-propulsion, improved internal combustion, made vehicles affordable through mass production, built infrastructure around them, and later pushed for safety, efficiency, and electrification.

## Research checklist and candidate milestones

| Era | Candidate milestone | Why it matters | Verification caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late 1700s | Nicolas-Joseph Cugnot's steam-powered road vehicle | Early attempt at a self-propelled road vehicle | Distinguish road-vehicle prototype from modern automobile; collision anecdotes need independent confirmation |
| 1800s | Experiments with steam, electric, and internal-combustion propulsion | Multiple competing technologies existed | Avoid saying all cars began with gasoline |
| 1885–1886 | Karl Benz's Patent-Motorwagen | Influential purpose-built gasoline automobile | Explain the patent/working-vehicle distinction; don't claim universal sole inventor |
| 1888 | Bertha Benz's long-distance drive | Demonstrated practical use and exposed infrastructure challenges | Verify route, repairs, and pharmacy-fuel details before dramatizing |
| Early 1900s | Ford Model T and moving assembly-line manufacturing | Reduced cost and widened access | Model T launched in 1908; moving assembly line introduced later; distinguish these dates |
| Mid-1900s | Safety engineering and road infrastructure | Changed everyday driving and consequences | Select specific verified examples and avoid claiming one person invented all safety features |
| Late 1900s–present | Electronics, emissions rules, hybrid/electric vehicles, driver assistance | Explains today's design and trade-offs | Separate technology invention from mass adoption; don't suggest all modern cars are autonomous |

**Potential comic moment:** An elegant stickman inventor proudly points at a rattling early vehicle; a horse watches skeptically. This is a *visual joke*, not a claim about a recorded incident.

**Important:** Cugnot's often-repeated “first car crash” story is disputed; do not present it as verified. Early electric vehicles predate today's EV resurgence.

## Three detailed image/motion prompt examples

### Shot 01 — The impossible comparison (modern hook)

**Narration draft:** “Today, cars can help steer, park, and warn us about danger. But the first inventors had a much simpler goal: make a carriage move without a horse.”

**Image prompt:** 2D cinematic storybook illustration, present-day urban street at golden hour. A compact contemporary car with mechanically plausible wheels, windows, headlights, and visible driver-assistance sensor shapes occupies the right foreground. On the left, an expressive dark-outline stickman narrator in a mustard scarf points at the vehicle, one eyebrow raised. In the softly detailed background: crosswalk, traffic signal, glass storefronts, trees, distant traffic, all people represented only as 2D stickmen. Strong silhouette, playful documentary tone, warm sunlight, teal-and-amber restrained palette, hand-inked texture. Wide 16:9 framing with clean space at top-left for the title. No photorealistic humans, no illegible text, no logos, no extra limbs, no warped car geometry.

**Motion prompt:** The narrator begins facing the car with one arm lowered, raises the arm to point, and turns to camera. The car's headlights glow subtly; traffic in the background moves gently. End with narrator's hand extended toward the front wheel for the rewind transition. No narration instructions.

### Shot 06 — Steam-era ambition (late 1700s)

**Narration draft:** “Long before gasoline engines ruled the road, experimenters tried steam power. The machines were clever, heavy, and not exactly built for a quick grocery run.”

**Image prompt:** 2D historical illustrated scene inspired by late-18th-century French engineering workshops and roads. A simple expressive stickman inventor in a period waistcoat, tricorne-style hat, and rolled sleeves stands beside an early heavy steam-powered road vehicle with large wooden wheels, bulky boiler assembly, and exposed mechanical structure; historically plausible form, no modern rubber tires or sleek steering wheel. Cobblestone foreground, stone workshop buildings, tools and wooden crates in midground, distant countryside, overcast morning light with soft steam, desaturated ochre and slate-blue palette, ink-and-paper texture. Three-quarter low camera angle emphasizing weight and experimental ingenuity; space for a date card added later. All humans are stickmen. No photorealistic faces, no modern engine parts, no invented text, no watermark.

**Motion prompt:** From a steady standing pose, the inventor turns a valve, a small plume of steam rises, and the vehicle nudges forward a short distance. The inventor steps back in surprise, ending at the right edge of frame. Preserve wooden-wheel geometry and period clothing; no exaggerated crash or implied historical accident.

### Shot 15 — Production changes everything (early 1900s)

**Narration draft:** “The real breakthrough wasn't only getting cars to work. It was finding ways to build enough of them that ordinary families could dream of owning one.”

**Image prompt:** Richly detailed 2D ink-and-paper illustration of an early-20th-century automobile factory assembly line. Rows of era-correct black Model T-style automobiles at different assembly stages, with plausible chassis, wheels, and open bodywork. Multiple distinct 2D stickman workers in period work shirts and caps, each performing a specific assembly task; no realistic human bodies. Foreground worker fitting a wheel, midground chassis on line, background factory windows, belts, structural steel, period industrial equipment. Wide cinematic 16:9 view, diagonal composition leading into the distance, warm industrial sunlight and muted graphite/sepia palette. Leave space for a small date label. No modern robots, no modern safety vests, no extra limbs, no garbled text.

**Motion prompt:** A stickman worker begins holding a wheel, aligns it with a chassis, and secures it while the assembly line advances slowly. End with the worker stepping back to reveal the fitted wheel. Maintain period factory layout and car geometry; no narration in motion prompt.
